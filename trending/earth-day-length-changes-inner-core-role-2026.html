Why Earth’s Day Length Changes: The Role of the Inner Core

📅 Trending on September 29, 2026 ⏱ 3 min read 🏷 Science / Earth

Earth’s day is not always exactly 24 hours. Scientists have known for years that the length of a day can change by a few milliseconds over decades. A new study now points to the cause deep inside the planet.

What the Study Found

Researchers looked at how Earth’s solid inner core moves. The inner core is a ball of solid metal at the very center of the planet. It does not spin at exactly the same speed as the rest of Earth.

Because the inner core is not a perfect sphere, and because the rocky mantle above it has uneven mass, gravity creates a pull between them. This gravitational pull acts like a slow twisting force, or torque. When the inner core shifts slightly out of line, gravity tries to pull it back into place. That tug affects the rotation of the mantle and the surface we live on.

A Push-and-Pull Battle

Other forces work against this gravitational pull. These include electromagnetic effects and physical contact at the boundary between the liquid outer core and the mantle. The result is a kind of tug-of-war deep inside Earth.

The gravitational force is the main driver of the long-term changes in day length. The opposing forces limit how much the day can lengthen or shorten. The small difference between these forces explains the observed shifts of a few milliseconds over about 60 to 70 years.

Why It Matters

These tiny changes do not affect daily life. People never notice a day that is a few thousandths of a second longer or shorter. But precise clocks, GPS systems, and scientific measurements do track them.

The finding helps solve a long-standing puzzle. Scientists knew that angular momentum moves between Earth’s core and mantle, but they were not sure exactly how. This gravitational link with the inner core provides a clear explanation that matches decades of data.

Looking Deeper

The study also suggests the inner core itself can slowly change shape over years. This paints a more dynamic picture of Earth’s deepest layers than many people expected.

The research was published in the journal Nature. It gives a clearer view of the hidden processes that keep our planet spinning at a slightly uneven rate.
